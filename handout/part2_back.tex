\heading{Part 2 activity: audit an analysis}{45 minutes \hfill At minute 40, finish your log (paper is fine)}
{\emergencystretch=3em\textbf{The case.} A member of your team gives you this notebook and write-up of a statistical analysis of the public Palmer Penguins data (344 penguins, three species, three islands). How can you use an LLM to augment your critical thinking and check the analysis? It contains deliberate problems of different difficulty. Finding every one is not the goal.\par}

{\emergencystretch=3em\textbf{Set up (5 to 10 min).} Get \code{workshop2_activity.zip} from \href{https://github.com/dowlinglab/claude-for-researchers/releases/latest}{\code{github.com/dowlinglab/}\allowbreak\code{claude-for-researchers/}\allowbreak\code{releases/latest}} (under Assets) and unzip it. Open \code{audit-project/} in Claude; in a chat app, attach \code{writeup.md}, the notebook, and \code{data/README.md}. No references to save: the prompt links to them.\par}

\promptbox{\textbf{Starter prompt.} ``A teammate sent me this notebook and write-up. I want to audit the analysis, not fix it. Read the write-up, the notebook, and the data documentation, and change nothing. List each substantive claim and where it appears. Then give each claim a status (supported, questionable, incorrect, or insufficient evidence). Say whether you checked it against the notebook's output, the raw data, or a reference, cite the section you relied on, and tell me what you could not open or check. References: the files in \code{references/pdfs/}, or these links: \code{openintro-ims.netlify.app}, \code{itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook}, \code{amstat.org/asa/files/pdfs/p-valuestatement.pdf}.''}

\promptbox{\textbf{OPTIONAL Step 0. If you are comfortable with Git and Python; otherwise skip it. You are not behind.} A notebook is awkward for an agent to edit and for you to review, because its diffs mix code, outputs, and images. Commit a clean baseline first, then move the analysis code into Python files and commit that separately, then audit. Ask Claude to inspect the project, propose the structure before changing anything, and keep the refactor separate from any statistical corrections. Give it at most 15 minutes.}

\par\vspace{5pt}{\large\bfseries\color{NDBlue}The audit, in order}\hfill{\small The starter prompt does Steps 1 to 3. Read the reply, then continue at Step 4.}\par
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=1pt]
\item \textbf{Inspect, change nothing.} Skim the write-up yourself, then ask Claude to summarize what was done and concluded.
\item \textbf{List the claims.} Ask for every substantive claim, with its location. Check the list for gaps.
\item \textbf{Audit against the references.} Ask for a status on each claim, a citation to the specific chapter, section, or principle, and what it says. Keep the reference separate from Claude's own reasoning.
\item \textbf{Audit the code too.} Ask whether the code does what the prose says and every number matches the output.
\item \textbf{Propose, do not apply.} Ask for each correction with its reason.
\item \textbf{Verify at least three findings yourself, of different kinds:} a number you recompute (a calculator or Excel is fine; ask Claude to quote the notebook cell), a citation you open, and one claim marked Supported that you try to break.
\item \textbf{Iterate.} If the audit looks shallow, change the prompt or add context. Try a fresh conversation and compare.
\item \textbf{Revise and review.} Apply corrections you accept, on a copy or a branch, and read the changes yourself.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Second pass:} ``Recompute every number independently.'' ``List each test's assumptions; do the data meet them?''
